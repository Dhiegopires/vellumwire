Nobody Notices Their Own Website Getting Slower

Your site doesn't feel slow to you, you've already loaded it a hundred times. Here's why speed quietly costs leads, and how to actually check.

~4 min read · July 28, 2026
Performance

You check your own site more than any visitor ever will, and that's exactly why you're the last person who'd notice it's gotten slow. Your browser has it cached. Your connection is probably faster than a prospect's phone on a job site. And you've already sat through the load a hundred times, so a few extra seconds don't register as a problem, they register as normal.

We already covered the more common version of this mistake: assuming your site's problem is how it looks, when the real problem is structural. Speed is the same mistake wearing a different disguise. It's structural, it's measurable, and the person who owns the site is structurally the worst-positioned person in the world to notice it's happening.

Websites Don't Get Slow All at Once

No one redesigns a site and makes it slow on purpose. It happens through addition, not a single decision. A booking widget gets added because a partner asked for it. A hero image gets swapped for a nicer one, uncompressed, because nobody thought to resize it first. A chat widget or review-plugin script gets installed for a campaign that ended a year ago and never gets removed, because removing it isn't anyone's job. Each of these, on its own, costs a fraction of a second. None of them look like a problem in isolation.

What they add up to isn't visible by looking at the homepage. It doesn't show up as a broken layout or a missing button. It shows up only in one place: how long the page actually takes to load on a device that isn't yours, on a connection that isn't yours, for a visitor who has never seen the site before and will not wait around to find out if it's worth it.

What the Slowdown Actually Costs

This isn't a design opinion, it's measured. Google, working with SOASTA, built machine-learning models on real-world mobile site data across 93 different page metrics, with a disclosed accuracy of 93% for predicting conversions and 96% for predicting bounces. The findings: as load time goes from one second to ten seconds, the probability of a mobile visitor bouncing rises 123%. As the number of elements on a page (text, images, scripts) goes from 400 to 6,000, the probability of conversion drops 95%. Mobile pages that loaded just one second faster saw conversion increases of up to 27%.

Google/SOASTA mobile site speed research. Machine-learning models built on real-world data, 93% to 96% disclosed prediction accuracy.

None of that means any specific reader's site will bounce or convert at exactly these rates. It's mechanism evidence, not a personal prediction. What it establishes is that the relationship between load time and lost visitors isn't a theory, it's been measured directly, and the direction only ever points one way: slower costs you people who never told you why they left.

The same rebuild also fixed a performance problem the case study itself names directly: a "performance liability" that undermined trust before the site was rebuilt with a clean, lightweight codebase, "fast load times, from the ground up."

We aren't going to reconstruct a story about exactly how Selo H's load time got to 4.8 seconds in the first place. That specific history isn't something we have on record, and inventing one would be exactly the kind of unsupported detail this blog doesn't publish. What's real and checkable is the before number, the after number, and the fact that a full technical rebuild, not a patch, is what closed the gap.

How to Actually Check, Instead of Guessing

This is the one part of "is my site slow" that doesn't come down to anyone's opinion, yours or an agency's. Load time is a number you can measure directly, on a real connection, today, without redesigning anything first. If you haven't measured it in the last few months, you don't currently know the answer, you're guessing based on how the site feels to you, and you've already read why that's the least reliable way to find out.
